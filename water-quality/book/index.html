    Ch 1 · Water Chemistry

    Water is a weird little molecule

    3D model · H₂Odrag to spin

    Oxygen pulls electrons harder than hydrogen, so the O end is slightly negative (δ−) and the H ends slightly positive (δ+). Opposite ends of neighboring molecules attract: that's a hydrogen bond.

    Hydrogen bonds explain water's superpowers: it absorbs lots of heat before warming up, dissolves salts easily, and its ice floats.

    Salinity: how salty is it?

    Open ocean salinity averages about 35 ppt (35 g of salt per kg of water, or 3.5%). Estuaries mix river water with seawater, so their salinity can be anything from 0.5 to 30 ppt.

    • Seawater freezes near −1.9 °C (salt lowers the freezing point).
    • Coral reefs like 32–40 ppt.
    • Pressure goes up 1 atm every 10 m of depth, plus 1 atm of air at the surface.
    • Units: 1 mg/L ≈ 1 ppm; 1% = 10 ppt.

    Ch 1 · Water Chemistry

    pH and the souring sea

    pH measures hydrogen ions (H⁺). Lower pH = more acidic. The scale is logarithmic: each 1 unit is a 10× change in H⁺.

    WaterTypical pH
    Surface ocean today≈ 8.1 (7.5–8.4)
    OK for marine life6.5–8.5
    OK for freshwater life6–9
    Natural rain≈ 5.6

    Ocean acidification

    The ocean has soaked up roughly a quarter to a third of the CO₂ people release. CO₂ + water makes carbonic acid, so surface pH has dropped about 0.1 since pre-industrial times. That sounds tiny, but 100.1 ≈ 1.26, so it's about 26% more H⁺.

    Aragonite saturation (Ω)

    Corals and shellfish build skeletons from aragonite (a form of CaCO₃). Ω tells you how easy that is.

    ΩMeaning for reefs
    > 4.0Optimal
    3.5–4.0Adequate
    3.0–3.5Marginal
    < 3.0Low
    < 1.0Corrosive: shells start dissolving

    Bonus numbers: surface seawater alkalinity ≈ 115 mg/L as CaCO₃; seawater conductivity ≈ 53 mS/cm.

    Ch 1 · Water Chemistry

    Dissolved oxygen: can the fish breathe?

    Fish breathe oxygen gas dissolved in water (DO), measured in mg/L. Two rules: cold water holds more O₂, and salty water holds less.

    DO at saturation (approx.)20 °C
    7.4 mg/L max
    DO (mg/L)What it means
    ≥ 5Supports most marine life
    4.8 / 2.3EPA criteria: growth / survival (Virginian Province)
    < 2Hypoxia (a "dead zone")
    ≈ 0Anoxia

    DO swings every day: photosynthesis adds O₂ in sunlight, and everything respires all night. DO is lowest just before dawn.

    Ch 1 · Water Chemistry

    Measuring oxygen: Winkler & BOD

    Winkler titration

    A classic chemistry method: you "fix" the oxygen in the bottle, then titrate with sodium thiosulfate.

    200 mL sample + 0.025 N thiosulfate → 1 mL titrant = 1 mg/L DO
    Worked example: 7.2 mL used
    1. Check the setup: 200 mL sample, 0.025 N titrant. ✔
    2. So each mL equals 1 mg/L.
    3. DO = 7.2 mg/L. Healthy water!

    BOD₅ (Biochemical Oxygen Demand)

    BOD tells you how much oxygen bacteria will eat up while breaking down organic stuff like sewage or dead algae. High BOD = trouble coming for fish.

    Incubate the bottle 5 days, 20 °C, in the dark. Dark, so algae can't photosynthesize and sneak extra oxygen in.

    BOD₅ = (D₁ − D₂) ÷ P
    D₁ = DO at start · D₂ = DO after 5 days · P = fraction of the bottle that is sample
    Worked example: diluted sample
    1. You fill a 300 mL bottle with 30 mL sample + dilution water, so P = 30 ÷ 300 = 0.10.
    2. D₁ = 8.4 mg/L, D₂ = 5.1 mg/L. Drop = 3.3 mg/L.
    3. BOD₅ = 3.3 ÷ 0.10 = 33 mg/L.
    4. Compare: clean water ≈ 1–2, treated sewage limit 30, raw sewage 100–400. This is a bit above the treatment limit.

    Ch 2 · Nutrients, Clarity & Bacteria

    Nitrogen & phosphorus: plant food

    Algae need nutrients like we need food. A little is good. Too much makes a bloom.

    Nitrogen (N)

    • Forms: nitrate NO₃⁻, nitrite NO₂⁻, ammonium, N₂ gas
    • Test: cadmium reduction turns it pink/magenta
    • Clean water: < 1 mg/L as N
    • Drinking limit: 10 mg/L nitrate-N
    • Limits coastal/marine growth

    Phosphorus (P)

    • Tests detect orthophosphate
    • Ascorbic acid test turns it blue (molybdenum blue)
    • No gas form; it rides on eroded soil
    • EPA: ≤ 0.05 mg/L P into lakes
    • Limits freshwater growth

    "As N" vs. "as NO₃": the unit trap

    Labs report nitrate either as the whole ion (NO₃) or as just the nitrogen inside it (NO₃-N). Convert with:

    NO₃ = 4.43 × NO₃-N  ·  PO₄ = 3.07 × PO₄-P
    Why 4.43?
    1. NO₃ mass = 14 + 3×16 = 62 g/mol. N alone = 14.
    2. 62 ÷ 14 = 4.43. Same idea for PO₄: 95 ÷ 31 = 3.07.

    Redfield ratio (what plankton are made of): C : N : P = 106 : 16 : 1.

    Nitrifying bacteria turn ammonium → nitrite (Nitrosomonas) → nitrate (Nitrobacter). Denitrifiers in low-oxygen mud turn nitrate back into N₂ gas.

    How a dead zone happens

    Eutrophication is a chain reaction. Tap Next step and watch the oxygen meter.

    Eutrophication chainStep 1 / 7

    Bottom DO
    Tricky test fact: during a bloom, daytime DO and pH go up (so much photosynthesis!). The crash comes at night and after the algae die.

    Low bottom oxygen also lets mud release phosphate, ammonium and H₂S, which feeds the next bloom. That's a positive feedback loop.

    Ch 2 · Nutrients, Clarity & Bacteria

    Turbidity: how cloudy is it?

    Turbidity is cloudiness from floating particles (mud, algae, sewage). Cloudy water blocks light, warms up, clogs gills and smothers the bottom.

    ToolUnit
    Nephelometer (light scattered at 90°)NTU
    Secchi disk (lowered until it vanishes)m
    Transparency tubecm
    Jackson candle (very muddy water)JTU

    Secchi depth = average of where the disk disappears going down and reappears coming up.

    k ≈ 1.7 ÷ Secchi depth (light attenuation, per m)
    1% light depth ≈ 2.7 × Secchi depth
    Worked example: Secchi = 3.4 m
    1. k = 1.7 ÷ 3.4 = 0.5 m⁻¹
    2. Light reaches 1% at about 2.7 × 3.4 ≈ 9.2 m. Seagrass deeper than that is in trouble.

    Solids

    TS = TSS + TDS
    TSS = caught on a filter · TDS = passes through
    TDS (mg/L) ≈ 0.67 × conductivity (µS/cm)

    Only reverse osmosis, distillation or ion exchange really remove TDS. Filtering and settling remove TSS. EPA's secondary drinking-water guideline for TDS is 500 mg/L.

    Ch 2 · Nutrients, Clarity & Bacteria

    Poop detectives: indicator bacteria

    We can't test for every germ, so we test for indicator bacteria that live in guts. If they're there, sewage or animal waste is too, and so might be germs that cause cholera, typhoid or hepatitis A.

    • Fecal coliforms grow at a hot 44.5 °C.
    • E. coli: best indicator for fresh water.
    • Enterococci: EPA's indicator for marine & estuary water.

    Membrane filtration count

    Pour water through a 0.45 µm filter, put it on m-FC medium at 44.5 °C for 24 h, then count the blue colonies. Ideal: 20–60 colonies. Over 200 = "too numerous to count" (TNTC).

    CFU/100 mL = colonies ÷ mL filtered × 100
    Worked example: 42 colonies from 30 mL
    1. 42 ÷ 30 = 1.4 colonies per mL
    2. × 100 = 140 CFU/100 mL
    3. Above the old recreation guideline (200)? No. But way above the shellfish limit (14 MPN/100 mL median).

    Guidelines

    UseLimit (per 100 mL)
    Marine beach, enterococci (geometric mean)35
    Fresh water, E. coli (geometric mean)126
    Shellfish harvest (median)14
    Treated drinking water, total coliform0

    Sampling: sterile bottle, don't rinse it, keep it below 10 °C, start within 8 hours.

    Ch 3 · Marine & Estuary Ecology

    Estuaries: where rivers meet the sea

    Estuaries are among the most productive places on Earth (about 1,500 g of plant matter per m² per year). Most energy flows through detritus, bits of dead plants.

    Salt marsh

    Low marsh: flooded every tide, Spartina alterniflora, fiddler crabs, ribbed mussels. High marsh: flooded only by spring/storm tides, Spartina patens. Invader: Phragmites.

    Mangroves

    Red: arching prop roots, babies sprout on the tree. Black: pneumatophores (snorkel roots) poke up, salt crystals on leaves. White: salt glands at leaf base.

    Seagrass

    Real flowering plants, not seaweed! Nursery for baby fish. Dies when water gets cloudy.

    Mudflats

    Black, rotten-egg-smelling mud = iron sulfides made by sulfate-reducing bacteria (they make H₂S).

    Oyster reefs

    One oyster filters up to 190 L/day. Chesapeake oysters are only about 1–2% of historic levels.

    Summer estuaries can stratify: warm fresh water floats on cold salty water, so oxygen can't mix down and bottom water goes hypoxic. Fall storms mix it again.

    Flushing time = volume ÷ inflow
    Ch 3 · Marine & Estuary Ecology

    Tides: the Moon's tug-of-war

    The Moon's gravity (plus a smaller pull from the Sun) makes tides. A tidal day is 24 h 50 min.

    PatternPer dayExample
    Semidiurnal2 highs, 2 lows, about equalU.S. Atlantic coast
    Diurnal1 high, 1 lowParts of the Gulf of Mexico
    Mixed2 highs, 2 lows, unequalU.S. Pacific coast
    Moon phase → tide range
    • Spring tides (biggest range): Sun, Earth, Moon in a line at new and full moon. Nothing to do with the season spring!
    • Neap tides (smallest range): at first and third quarter.
    • Flood tide = rising. Ebb tide = falling.

    Rocky shore zones

    From top to bottom: spray zone → high → middle → low intertidal → subtidal. Animals higher up must survive drying out.

    Energy pyramid calculatorbar width on a log scale
    %

    About 10% of energy moves up each level (real range 5–20%). The rest is lost as heat. An energy pyramid can never be upside down; biomass and number pyramids sometimes are in the ocean.

    Plankton

    • Phytoplankton (producers): diatoms (glass/silica shells), dinoflagellates, coccolithophores (CaCO₃ plates), cyanobacteria.
    • Zooplankton (consumers): copepods, krill.
    • Holoplankton drift their whole life; meroplankton are just babies (crab, fish larvae).
    • Diel vertical migration: zooplankton go down by day, up at night.
    Ch 3 · Marine & Estuary Ecology

    Counting critters: populations

    Mark and recapture

    Catch and tag some animals, let them go, catch again later and count how many have tags.

    N = (M × C) ÷ R
    M = marked first time · C = caught second time · R = recaptured with marks
    Mark–recapture calculator
    N ≈ 300

    Growth curves

    • Exponential (J-curve): no limits. Doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate (%).
    • Logistic (S-curve): levels off at carrying capacity K. Fastest growth at K/2.
    • Density-dependent limits (competition, disease) get stronger with crowding. Density-independent ones (storms, oil spills) don't care.

    Life strategies Div C State/Nat

    r-selected: tons of tiny babies (oysters, copepods). K-selected: few big babies, long lives (whales). Survivorship Type I (humans, whales), II (constant), III (most die young: oysters, most fish). Nice to know, but Division B tests focus elsewhere.

    Ch 3 · Marine & Estuary Ecology

    Pollution & the laws that fight it

    Point source

    One pipe you can point at: a factory outfall, a sewage plant.

    Non-point source

    Spread out: runoff from farms, lawns, streets. Now the bigger problem in the U.S.

    Biomagnification

    Some poisons stick in fat and never leave: methylmercury, DDT, PCBs. Each predator eats lots of prey, so the dose climbs up the food chain.

    phytoplankton 0.01 ppm Hg → … → orca ≈ 100 ppm (10,000× more)

    Other pollutants

    • Oil: natural seeps are the largest source worldwide. Spills: Exxon Valdez 1989 (~11 million gal), Deepwater Horizon 2010 (~134–206 million gal).
    • Metals: lead has no safe level; arsenic limit 10 µg/L.
    • Plastics: entangle, get eaten, carry other toxins.
    • Invasive species: often arrive in ships' ballast water and leave their predators behind.
    Load = concentration × flow (mg/L × m³/s = g/s)

    Laws timeline

    1972Clean Water Act (discharges into rivers, lakes, coasts); DDT banned in U.S.
    1974Safe Drinking Water Act (tap water)
    1979PCBs banned
    1990Oil Pollution Act → double-hulled tankers

    Ch 4 · Coral Reefs

    Corals are animals with roommates

    A coral is a colony of tiny animals called polyps (phylum Cnidaria, class Anthozoa, cousins of jellyfish). They build skeletons out of aragonite.

    Inside their tissue live algae called zooxanthellae. The algae photosynthesize and give the coral up to ~90% of its food energy. In return they get a safe home and the coral's CO₂ and nutrients.

    Bleaching

    Too-hot water stresses the coral and it kicks the algae out. The tissue turns see-through, so you see the white skeleton. A bleached coral is alive but starving. A coral eaten by crown-of-thorns has bare, dead skeleton.

    What reefs need

    18–30 °CClear, shallow water32–40 ppt salinityLow nutrientsHard surfaceΩ above ~3

    Reef types (Darwin's order)

    Fringing (hugs the shore, most common) → barrier (lagoon between it and shore) → atoll (ring around a sunken volcano).

    Reef zones

    Back reef/lagoon (closest to shore) → reef crest (waves break) → fore reef (most diverse).

    Reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor but hold about 25% of marine species. Biggest threats: warming and acidification, plus overfishing, runoff, sediment and crown-of-thorns outbreaks.

    Ch 5 · Salinometer (Part IV)

    Build a salt-meter

    At the event you'll get salt water between 1% and 10% (mass/volume) and must report its salinity to 0.1%. Your device must work in a 400–600 mL beaker.

    Option A: Hydrometer

    A sealed straw weighted at the bottom (clay, a BB). It floats higher in saltier water because salty water is denser. Mark a scale on the straw.

    Option B: Conductivity

    Battery + two electrodes + a meter. More salt = more ions = more current. Changes about 2% per °C, so watch temperature.

    Banned: anything programmable or WiFi-capable, laptops, tablets.

    Calibrate at home

    1. Make standards: % m/v = grams of salt per 100 mL. 50.0 g NaCl topped up to 500 mL = 10%.
    2. Dilute with C₁V₁ = C₂V₂. For 500 mL of 2% from 10% stock: V₁ = 2 × 500 ÷ 10 = 100 mL.
    3. Use at least 5 standards (like 1, 3, 5, 7, 10%).
    4. Graph reading vs. salinity, draw a best-fit line, read unknowns off it.

    Error detective

    ProblemHydrometerConductivity
    Sample warmer than calibrationreads lowreads high
    Sample colderreads highreads low
    Air bubble stuck on itreads highreads low
    Float soaked up water / salt crustreads low–

    Accuracy target: ±1% at Regionals, ±0.5% at State & Nationals. Read a hydrometer at eye level, bottom of the meniscus.
